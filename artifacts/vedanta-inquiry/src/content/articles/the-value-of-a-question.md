**Sample article**

### Questions that ask for information

Some questions are practical. Where did I leave my keys? What time does the train arrive? They ask for information, and a correct answer lets us move on.

### Questions that invite examination
Self-inquiry works differently. It is less like finding a missing object and more like learning to see the room we are already in. “Who am I?” is not an invitation to invent a more impressive identity. It asks us to examine what we ordinarily take for granted.

That examination has a rhythm. We listen to a teaching. We consider whether it makes sense. Then we notice how it meets our own experience. The movement is from hearing, to reflection, to a recognition that becomes less dependent on someone else’s words.

### Staying with the question
If a question creates pressure to reach a special state, set that pressure down. A question can remain alive without being vague. Be precise about what you observe; be patient about what follows.

Try asking, “Is this thought the same as the one that came before it?” Or, “What makes this sensation known?” Small questions can loosen a large assumption.

Study is not measured by how quickly we can repeat a conclusion. It is measured by whether we are learning to look for ourselves.
