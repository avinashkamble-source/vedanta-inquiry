**Sample article**

We use the word *I* all day long. I am tired. I am glad you called. I remember that place. It is a small word carrying a remarkably large bundle: a body, a history, a set of preferences, a voice that seems to comment on everything.

Vedanta begins not by asking you to believe a different story about yourself, but by looking closely at the one already in use.

Notice that your body is known to you. Its sensations change through the day, and you can describe them: warm, restless, hungry. Thoughts are known too. One arrives, stays for a moment, and gives way to another. Even your sense of being a particular person has a shape—it gathers around memories, relationships, and names.

The inquiry is simple, though not necessarily easy: *what is it that knows these changing things?*

This is not a puzzle to solve with a clever answer. “Awareness” can become just another word to collect. Instead, pause with the actual experience of reading this sentence. There is the sentence, there may be a thought about it, and there is the fact that both are present to you.

For now, that is enough. The tradition asks for patient attention, not an immediate conclusion. We begin with what can be examined directly.

### A small practice

For a few breaths, let sounds, sensations, and thoughts arrive without sorting them into *me* and *not me*. Then ask, gently: **What is present to all of this?** Leave the question open. Inquiry is often most useful before it becomes an answer.
